## What this stage is

Every GPU-hour is a kilowatt-hour first. A data centre needs firm power: electricity available every hour of the year, not the average output of a wind farm. In the United States that means gas-fired plants, nuclear, and increasingly solar paired with batteries, plus generators on the site itself while a grid connection is awaited.

## How it works

A generator is bought by the megawatt of capacity and paid for by the megawatt-hour it produces. Two numbers describe it: the cost to build, in dollars per kilowatt, and the cost to run, in dollars per megawatt-hour, which is mostly fuel. A combined-cycle gas plant is the workhorse of firm supply; a simple-cycle turbine is cheaper to build and dearer to run, so it serves peaks. Nuclear has the highest build cost and the lowest fuel cost, which is why retired reactors are being restarted and existing ones uprated under long contracts with data-centre buyers.

The constraint is not demand. It is the order book for large gas turbines, which three manufacturers control, the delivery of fuel to the site, and the years of permitting and construction. A plant ordered today is a plant that runs several years from now.

## What to watch

- Planned capacity additions by fuel and year, and how much of it is firm.
- Turbine order books and quoted delivery dates.
- Power purchase agreements signed by data-centre operators: price, term, source.
- Wholesale power prices in the regions where campuses cluster.
- On-site generation announcements: they signal a grid connection that could not be had in time.

## How it connects

Megawatts of generation become megawatts delivered only through the grid stage. Downstream, the price of a megawatt-hour is the largest cash cost of a GPU-hour after the GPU itself.
